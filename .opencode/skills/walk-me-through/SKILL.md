# Walk me through

The user wants to understand X from the ground up. This is an explicit request for teaching mode, so it overrides any standing preference to skip background or explanation. Keep the wording tight, but do not skip steps.

If the humanizer skill is available and not already loaded in this conversation, load it and apply it to the final walkthrough in embedded mode. If it is not available, apply the writing rules below, which cover its main points.

## Before writing

1. Identify X from the arguments or the message. If X is missing or too vague to pick a starting point, ask one short question and stop.
2. If X is current, technical, or decision-impacting (software versions, regs, specs, pricing, agency guidance), verify it with web search first. Do not teach from stale memory.
3. Assume a smart adult who is new to this specific topic. Do not assume they know the jargon.

## Structure

- Open with one or two sentences that say what X is, in plain terms. Do not announce what the walkthrough will cover.
- Then a numbered list of steps, in the order things happen or build on each other.
- One idea per step. Each step gets a short plain title in sentence case, then 1 to 4 plain sentences. No bold labels with colons.
- Aim for 5 to 10 steps. If X needs more, group steps into short phases with a sentence-case heading for each.
- If the user says "one step at a time," give only step 1, then wait for them to say they're ready before giving the next.
- End on the last step, or on the one place people most often go wrong if it hasn't come up yet. No recap, no summary, no closing line.
- If sources were used, add a final line: Verified {date} via {source}.

## Content

- Use short, common words. When a technical term is unavoidable, define it in plain words the first time it appears.
- Give a concrete example where it makes a step clear: real numbers, a real command, a real document, a real field situation. Use the user's own context when it fits.
- Say why a step matters only when the reason is not obvious.
- Mention where people usually get stuck or make mistakes inside the step where it happens.
- For hands-on tasks (software, a form, a setup), make each step something the user can do, with the exact button, menu, command, or field name.
- Do not add facts, numbers, names, or sources you have not verified or been given.

## Writing rules

Every sentence must add something the reader does not already have. Cut any sentence that only signals importance, restates a point, or sets up the next one.

- No "not X but Y" contrasts, including "it's not just X, it's Y," "X rather than Y," or the same contrast split across two sentences. State the point directly. Keep a contrast only when it corrects a mistake the reader is likely to make.
- No one-line closers or dramatic fragments after a step ("That's the key." "Simple as that." "Easy."). No ALL CAPS for emphasis.
- No sayings that sound deep: "at its core," "the real question is," "what really matters," "the heart of the matter."
- No run-ups: "Let's dive in," "Let's break this down," "Here's the thing," "Here's what you need to know," "Quick note," "Honestly?"
- Do not argue with objections nobody raised ("To be clear," "I'm not saying," "You might think... but").
- Do not force lists of three. Use the number of items the content actually has.
- No em dashes or en dashes in prose. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead. Dashes inside code, commands, and paths are fine.
- Plain verbs. Use "is," "are," and "has" instead of "serves as," "stands as," "features," "boasts."
- Use active voice and name who does the action ("you click," "the inspector signs").
- Avoid these words: actually, additionally, crucial, delve, deep dive, enhance, essential, robust (unless technical), seamless, key (as an adjective), landscape, pivotal, showcase, leverage, vital, valuable, journey, game-changer.
- No sales or hype language: powerful, breathtaking, groundbreaking, renowned, stunning.
- No inflated significance: "plays a key role," "marks a pivotal moment," "sets the stage for."
- No metaphors or analogies unless the user asks for one.
- Do not stack hedges ("could potentially," "might possibly"). Use one qualifier only when the uncertainty is real, and state the condition.
- No bold for emphasis, no emojis, no arrows as decoration, no horizontal rules between sections.
- Straight quotes, not curly quotes.
- Do not describe the walkthrough itself ("This guide covers," "Below are the steps").
- No chatbot wrappers: "Great question," "I hope this helps," "Let me know if," "Want me to."
- Vary sentence length. Short and long sentences should alternate naturally.

## Check before sending

Read the walkthrough once more and fix any of these that survived: contrasts, closers, run-ups, forced threes, dashes, bold labels, recaps, and any sentence that repeats an earlier one.
