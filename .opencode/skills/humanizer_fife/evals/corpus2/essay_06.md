# The Four-Day Workweek: What the Data Actually Says

The four-day workweek isn't a fringe experiment anymore — it's a global conversation backed by real trials. Companies that cut hours without cutting pay are reporting results that challenge a century of assumptions about productivity.

Advocates point to three core benefits: focus, retention, and wellbeing. Fewer meeting hours force prioritization. Shorter weeks retain talent. Rested employees simply do better work.

But what truly determines success isn't the calendar — it's the redesign. When companies merely compress forty hours into four, burnout follows. When they eliminate low-value meetings and redundant processes, the math works.

It's important to note the honest caveats. Not every industry can participate; hospitals and factories face real constraints. Customer expectations require management. Some early trials have quietly failed.

The evidence so far suggests a careful conclusion: for knowledge work, the four-day week is viable when organizations treat it as a redesign project rather than a perk. The question is no longer whether it can work — it's which organizations are willing to do the work it requires.
